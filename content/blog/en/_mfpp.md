## Introduction {#introduction}

This book is different from other marketing books.

First, it is written by a web developer. The job of a web developer is to solve problems, and, more specifically, to break bigger problems into smaller ones. In this book, I break down the global concept of marketing into smaller, actionable items.

Second, it is a short book. On purpose. As a reader, I am constantly upset to read non-fiction books of hundreds of pages that could perfectly hold in a dozen. So, this book sticks to the necessary words. In a radical and adamant way. This also makes it a dense book.

Third, it is a very structured book. The ideas and concepts explored here are organized by levels, with a top-down approach. You see the bigger picture before getting into the details. The chapters are also ordered in a progressive and interdependent way.

Finally, this book is meant to be a reference book. A book you revisit regularly. It is the result of years of trials, errors and thought about how to do marketing in a precise way. My goal is to provide a crystal-clear vision of what is marketing and what marketers (should) do.

Throughout the book, I assume that you have an offering and that you are the one in charge of marketing it.

## 1. Marketing and marketers {#marketing-and-marketers}

### 1.1. What is marketing? {#what-is-marketing}

#### A clear and simple definition

Marketing is the art of persuading people to use your offering.

Let's see what some of these words imply.

#### Offering

Your offering is what you have to offer: your product(s), your service(s) or combination(s) of both.

#### People

The term people is misleading, because marketing is clearly not about persuading everybody. People are first and foremost the targets of your offering: the ones who are interested or might be interested by it.

You can categorize the targets of your offering based on how far they are from using it **for the first time**. This is also referred as the **temperature** of your targets. The closer they are from using your offering for the first time, the warmer they are. For any offering, you can usually categorize your targets into 2 different groups.

1. **The prospects**. They are the potential users of your offering. They are the ones you need to persuade for the first time. There are 2 types of prospects: **anonymous** and **identified**. Identified prospects (or leads) are prospects who agreed to give you their e-mail address, their phone number, or any other way to contact them. The anonymous are the ones you cannot contact directly, because they didn't leave you any contact information to do so.

2. The **users**. They are the people who have already used your offering at least once. There can be 2 types of users for an offering: **paying** and **non-paying**. The paying users, or the customers, can be very different from the non-paying users. For instance, if we consider clothes for newborns, the customers are the parents and the non-paying users are the babies. The distinction between paying and non-paying users is also relevant for intermediation-based offerings (for example marketplaces) and for ad-based offerings. The paying users of Instagram are the advertisers, while the non-paying ones are the "Instagrammers". It is much easier to promote offerings that only have paying users than those that have paying and non-paying ones (it's at least twice more work).

#### Persuading

Persuading requires information. You can persuade people to do something only by giving them information, or to be more accurate, by communicating. The way people receive information, or the context (How? When? Where?), is as important as the information itself (What?). Persuading is communicating the right information to the right people at the right time, and it all depends on the context.

#### Art

As an art, marketing requires creativity. You must be creative about the information you deliver (messages) and the ways you deliver it (contexts).

### 1.2. What do marketers do? {#what-do-marketers-do}

Now that we have seen what marketing is, let's see what marketers do.

Marketing is a looping process. Marketers repeat 2 fundamental tasks:

1. Choose favorable contexts to reach their targets.
2. Craft optimized messages to deliver them in context.

## 2. Contexts {#contexts}

The first task of a marketer is to choose favorable contexts to reach the targets of an offering.

### 2.1. What is a context? {#what-is-a-context}

A context is the setting in which information, directly or indirectly relevant to your offering, can reach your targets. It is a combination of how, when and where you give information. A context is the way of exposing information so it can reach your ideal targets as much as possible.

A context is more or less favorable to your offer depending on the attention the targets pay to your message when it is delivered.

It all boils down to knowing the activities of your targets, and more specifically their characteristics and habits.

### 2.2. The characteristics and habits of your targets {#the-characteristics-and-habits-of-your-targets}

#### What characteristics and habits to look for?

The characteristics and habits of people usually fall into 1 of the following categories:

1. The biological identity. In particular:

   - The age and stage of life: infancy, toddlerhood, childhood, adolescence, post-adolescence, adulthood/parenthood, seniority.
   - The gender: male, female, other.
   - The ethnicity.
   - The health and the possible disabilities.
2. The personality. In particular:

   - The beliefs.
   - The behavior and emotional habits: see [Patrick Gunkel's list of 638 primary personal traits](http://ideonomy.mit.edu/essays/traits.html?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}.
3. The activities. In particular:

   - The activities that bring them revenue.
   - The activities that bring them pleasure or entertain them.
   - The activities that bring them pain.
4. The places of interest. In particular:

   - The place(s) of residence (where people sleep).
   - The place(s) of work or study.
   - The place(s) of hobby.

Note that there is an incremental influence between these categories:

- The biological identity of an individual influences who he is (his personality).
- The biological identity and the personality influence what he does (the activities).
- The biological identity, the personality, the activities influence where he goes and how he commutes (the places of interest).

#### How to find the characteristics and habits of your targets?

There are 3 different ways to do so. You can:

1. make assumptions, by relying on your best-inspired intuition and on your most-accurate observation. In other words, you make educated guesses about the characteristics and habits of your "ideal" prospects (also called personas). This is where your personal curiosity and curation makes the difference. This has something to do with the philosophical german concept of [zeitgeist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeitgeist?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}.
2. use data and statistics from reports published by market and consumer research companies. Just to name a few: [Statista](https://www.statista.com/?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}, [IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}, [Mintel](https://www.mintel.com/?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}, [Global Market Insights](https://www.gminsights.com/?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}, [Global Industry Reports](https://www.globalindustryreports.com/?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}, [Market Research Reports](https://www.marketresearchreports.com/?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}.
3. focus on the characteristics and habits you already observed in the users of your offering (if you already have some). If the users of your offering are satisfied with it, then similar people, with the same characteristics and habits, may also be interested. As a marketer, you want to discover and use these virtuous circles as soon as possible. The key is to spot meaningful patterns among your users.

## 3. Messages {#messages}

The second fundamental task of a marketer is to craft optimized messaged to deliver them in context. in this chapter, we explore useful concepts related to messages.

### 3.1. What is a message? {#what-is-a-message}

A message is a format (set of information) you deliver to your targets. There are 4 possible formats for a message:

1. Speech (live or recorded).
2. Text.
3. Image (usually with some text).
4. Video.

Whenever you can, try to use the video format, which is by far the most effective one. The expensive super bowl ads and the exisitence of shopping channels are the proof of it.

### 3.2. What are the components of a message? {#what-are-the-components-of-a-message}

A message, no matter its format, can be broken down into 2 types of components.

1. The branding components.
2. The topic components.

The branding components are basic communication bricks you can re-use in all of your messages. They make your messages belong together. There are 3 major branding components you should have ready to use at any time:

1. Your value proposition.
2. Your brand name.
3. Your logo.

Let's detail each one.

### 3.3. Value proposition {#value-proposition}

#### Definition

A value proposition (VP) or unique value proposition (UVP) is a clear, concise, precise, powerful, and easy-to-understand sequence of words aiming at:

- Describing the (promised) value of your offering.
- Establishing the main benefit for your users.
- Arousing the reader's interest.
- Setting your offering apart from the competition.

Value propositions can also be called selling propositions (SPs), unique selling propositions (USPs) or branding messages (BMs). Ideally, a value proposition is also easy to remember. In this case, the value proposition is a slogan, that your targets can repeat without effort. Slogans can be made easy to remember with intoned speech (see examples of Geico and Red Bull below).

#### Examples

Here are some examples of good value propositions:

- Go ahead, forget your passwords (1Password).
- Know what happens to your documents after you hit send (Attach).
- Save time managing social media for your business (Buffer).
- Simply great design for you and your team (Canva).
- Handcrafted burgers and fries since 1986 (Five Guys).
- Professional translation within hours (Gengo).
- 15 minutes could save you 15 percent or more on car insurance ([Geico](https://vimeo.com/754387509/b5f126549e?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}).
- Beautiful presentations without the struggle (Haiku Deck).
- Luxury included vacation for two people in love (Sandals).
- Democracy dies in darkness (The Washington Post).
- Accounting software for business owners who dislike bookkeeping (Less Acounting).
- Autoglass repair, Autoglass replace (Autoglass).
- Red Bull gives you wiiings ([Red Bull](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vp6FkPkL9z4&utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}).
- The first bank you will love (N26).
- Better ingredients, better pizza (Papa John's).
- All your video needs (Vimeo).
- Company email signatures that work (Xink).
- Schedule meetings without the back and forth (Pick).
- Create engaging infographics and reports in minutes (Infogram).

### 3.4. Brand name {#brand-name}

#### The power of brands

In the long term, you want your brand name to be the shortest value proposition you can have for your offering.

Just by hearing or seeing your brand, people should automatically associate your offering with it. No need to explain what Domino's, Starbucks or Tesla offer to their customers. These brand names have become the simplest way to designate their offering. The same goes with Google, which has become a verb. If I tell you to google something, you know what to do.

#### Good brand names

For your brand name to encapsulate your offering, you must come up with a good brand name. A good brand name is:

- Easily memorable. Simple, short (ideally 3 to 4 syllables maximum), easy to pronounce (in several languages ideally), easy to spell and easy to write.
- Distinctive. It should be different from what already exists, otherwise people will mix up the names or won't remember what you do.
- Not too specific. A very specific brand name is kind of a trap. You don't how your offering will evolve. It may change completely from your original plan, or you may want to diversify. If you name your restaurant "Delicious Hot Dogs", be sure that your offering will be mainly about hot dogs, otherwise it won't make much sense.
- Protectable. You should be able to legally protect your brand name if you want to, by registering it as a trademark.

If you're a freelancer or an independent worker, your brand name can be your first and last name. It is usually quite convenient: your face, your name, your brand.

#### Letter case of a brand name

They are 4 possible letter cases for a brand name:

- Camel case. 1 word with at least 2 capital letters. Example: FedEx, ConvertKit, TurboTax.
- Proper case. Sereval words, each one begining with a capital letter. Example: Mad Mimi, Red Bull, Less Acounting.
- Capitalized. 1 word, with 1 capital letter as the first letter. Example: Squarespace, Cloudflare, Canva.
- Lowercased. 1 or several words, with only lowercased letters.
- Partially lowercased. 1 word, with the first letter lowercased, and other letters capitalized. Example: easyJet.

Choose 1 letter case for your brand and stick to it for all your documents and supports.

#### Domain name

- Ideally, choose a brand name for which you can get the same domain name. If you can't, you still have the possibility to use another domain name that includes your brand name. For example, for many years, DropBox used the domain name getdropbox.com, before acquiring the domain name dropbox.com.
- I would recommend not to add any domain name extension to your brand name. The brand should be GreatGiraffe, not GreatGiraffe.com.

### 3.5. Logo {#logo}

A logo is the visual representation of your brand in the form of an image.

#### Types

There are 3 types of logos, from the most simple to the most complex:

1. Wordmark.
2. Wordmark with a separate design.
3. Wordmark incorporated within a larger design.

Here are some examples below:

![Types of logos](/images/logo-types.png)

The first column contains wordmarks. The second column contains wordmarks with separate designs on the left. The third column contains complex logos with the wordmarks inside larger designs.

While you can use several types of logos in your messages, I recommend choosing 1 type and 1 type only. The most convenient type is the wordmark alone, as it is the easiest one to insert in documents and supports of all kinds.

#### Shapes and dimensions

There are 2 possible shapes for a logo:

1. Square.
2. Rectangle or landscape.

In both cases, the bigger your logo is, the better it is. I recommend a width of at least 5000 pixels. That way, your logo can be used on large formats such as billboards.

#### Extensions

A logo is a digital file than can have 3 possible formats:

1. A vector format. Vector formats can be enlarged without losing quality. This format is usually used to create the others formats of logos. The most common vector formats are:.eps,.svg,.ai,.psd, or sketch.
2. A transparent format:.png. This is practical if you want your logo to fit with a specific background color.
3. A non-transparent format:.jpg, with a ddefault white background color.

### 3.6. The topic components {#the-topic-components}

Apart from having branding components, a message can also provide specific information about:

1. The benefits of your offering.
2. A limited-time offer: gift or discount.
3. A reward, obtainable through a gamification process: a referral/loyalty program or a contest.
4. Your users, through reviews, testimonials and case studies.
5. Something else: a topic directly or indirectly related to your offering.

## 4. What makes marketing challenging? {#challenges}

In this chapter, we explore some common topics any marketer scratches his head on, sooner or later.

### 4.1. Trust {#trust}

People will want to use your offering only if they can trust you. They will be able to trust you only if the information they receive about you, either directly from you or indirectly from someone else, seems trustworthy. How do you inspire trust?

#### Technics to inspire trust

I would suggest using at least 1 of these technics when crafting your messages:

1. Be honest/transparent. In particular, favor storytelling emphasizing emotions and feelings.
2. Be simple and clear.
3. Under-promise, over-deliver.
4. Be remarkable. *Be remarkable* is a concept that Seth Godin explores in his book Purple Cow. In a nutshell: have the audacity to be and to act differently from others. Embrace your originality and your uniqueness.
5. Treat people the way you would like to be treated.

#### Funnels

The most efficient way to inspire trust is to build it, bit by bit. You can do this by setting up a sequence of steps or a funnel. Before your prospects can trust you enough to use your offering straight away, especially if it is high-priced, you make them go through intermediary steps. Each step of the funnel is designed to provide incremental value to your prospects so that they can have an increased level of trust towards you and your offering. The first step of the funnel is usually giving something free: a piece of content, a demo, a call. The following steps provide more value, usually by requiring increasing amounts of money.

Here is an example of an evergreen funnel to sell software:

1. Publish articles regularly on your blog with a link to a weekly free webinar.
2. Host the webinar and at the end let people book free demos of the software.
3. At the end of each demo, offer a time-limited discount for the software.

Here is an example of an evergreen upsell funnel:

1. Run an ad on Facebook to offer a free e-book.
2. At the end of the e-book, link to a $19 guide.
3. At the end of the guide, link to a $199 consultation call.
4. At the end of the call, list the benefits of your $999 coaching program.

### 4.2. The effective frequency {#the-effective-frequency}

#### The optimal number of exposures

In an ideal world, the effective frequency would be the optimal number of exposures for a given message to make people act. In the real world, an effective frequency rarely exists.

You might have heard for example about the rule of 7: a prospect needs to hear or see a brand at least 7 times before they act. Well, the number 7 is a myth. There is absolutely no logical or empirical proof of the effectiveness of such a frequency.

The fundamental truth is that repetition of information is necessary but excessive repetition is counterproductive. You must repeat yourself. You have to remind people about your brand and your offering several times, but without saturating their attention, boring them or getting on their nerves.

#### The levels of relief

The number of exposures your audience needs before they act depends, first and foremost, on how relieving your offering is for them. For example, a business offering you instant access to electricity in your home if you're out of power can be considered as providing a high level of relief. On the other hand, a store selling jewels might be considered as providing a low level of relief.

The level of relief is quite subjective. It is perceived differently from one individual to another. However, there is an inverse correlation between the perceived level of relief of an offering and the number of exposures a prospect needs before he or she acts. As an example, the distribution could look something like this:

1. High relief: 1 to 2 exposures.
2. Moderate relief: 3 to 7 exposures.
3. Low relief: 8 to 100+ exposures (much harder to forecast).

### 4.3. The difficulty of communicating nowadays {#the-difficulty-of-communication-nowadays}

1 of the main tasks of marketers is to communicate to their target audience.

But, nowadays, we live in an era of over-communication, saturated with information.

Getting noticed in an ocean of information of all sorts is the paramount challenge of communication. Even if your communication is perfectly tailored for very specific and small target groups, you still will have to fight for people's time and attention.

1 way to do this is also by crafting remarkable messages. They help you stand out from the noise, and they also inspire trust as seen above. But beware of too much originality, as it can trigger backlash. Once again, it's a thin line to walk.

### 4.4. The perfect marketing team {#the-perfect-marketing-team}

What type of talent and skills do you need in your marketing team? Let's think in terms of roles. Note that a role can be endorsed by several people, and an individual can also endorse several roles.

The perfect marketing team should include:

- a writer/copywriter, to write great text.
- a graphic designer, to pick great images and create great visuals (including presentations).
- an analyst/strategist, to choose the targets of the offering, to pick the appropriate contexts, to guide the production of messages and to measure the results of the marketing campaigns. This role requires:

  - curiosity.
  - good analytical skills to measure and analyze the results.
  - good communication skills for overseeing the crafting of messages.
- an investigator, to collect data about the users. This role requires open-mindedness and empathy. In particular, an investigator:

  - reads market and consumer reports.
  - converses with the users to figure out their characteristics and habits.

Additionally:

- a photographer, to take great pictures of your offering (if need be).
- a sound engineer to produce great audio formats.
- video technicians to produce great videos: director, director of photography, camera operators, camera assistants, producers.
- Specialists/consultants to get help with context-specific tasks. For example:

  - a social media manager to create and publish posts (context: community platforms).
  - an advertising agency for writing the script, drawing the storyboard, and producing a TV commercial (context: broadcasts).

Freelancers and agencies are of great help for complementing the roles that are not in-house.

### 4.5. Marketing takes time, is risky and is uncertain {#marketing-takes-time-is-risky-and-is-uncertain}

The current era is super keen on quick tips and effortless "growth" hacks, but the reality is this: establishing a brand takes years. This makes sense because there are no possible shortcuts for inspiring trust.

Your very first marketing campaign can, of course, bring results as soon as it launches. But it's only by experimenting different marketing campaigns over time that you will be able to pass significant thresholds for your business. This implies taking risks at some point.

Finally, humans are irrational creatures. They permanently judge the information they receive, not only in in a conscious and objective way, but also in an unconscious and subjective way. This makes marketing an unpredictable process because people have cognitive biases. It is useful as a marketer to know about cognitive biases These resources are a good starting point:

1. [What Is Cognitive Bias?](https://www.simplypsychology.org/cognitive-bias.html?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}, by Charlotte Ruhl (SimplyPsychology).
2. [50 Cognitive Biases in the Modern World](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/50-cognitive-biases-in-the-modern-world/?utm_source=pelicanclan.com&utm_medium=article){target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-primary"}, by Marcu Lu (Visual Capitalist).

### 4.6. The blurred lines between marketing, business, sales and customer service {#blurred-lines}

#### Marketing and business

Marketing can hardly be separated from the general topic of business. The purpose of a business is to make money, and efficient marketing precisely results in sales. We've seen in a previous point the different roles in a marketing team. There is 1 role that is as much a business role as it is a marketing one: it's the role of the analyst/strategist. He or she chooses favorable contexts to reach the targets. However, this task is so impactful for a business, particularly in terms of budget, that the role of the analyst/strategist is best endorsed by the one in charge of the business: the CEO. He or she should take full responsibility of these decisions.

Setting the price of your offering is also at the frontier between marketing and business. While prices can hardly be determined by the marketing team on its own, they do have an impact on marketing. Price is a paramount piece of information that determines the level of persuasion to deploy for your prospects.

#### Marketing and sales

Marketing and sales are sometimes seen as two very distinct activities, but salespeople are marketers. They do a specific subtype of marketing called direct marketing: their mission is to talk people into using an offering. However, most of the time, salespeople are not considered and do not even consider themselves to be part of the marketing team. But they are. Either they directly produce the material they need to persuade people, either they rely, partially or totally, on the "marketing" people to do so. Either way, sales and marketing can be considered like the two sides of the same coin. A sale is the direct result of efficient marketing, or at least it should be. Salespeople are marketing people.

#### Marketing and customer service

In the marketing team, the investigator is the one who benefits the most from the customer service, by getting direct feedback from the users. The role of the investigator in the marketing team can easily be considered as customer service. The customer service of a business has the privilege to get direct feedback from the users, so the investigator is somehow required to do customer service.

### 4.7. How to measure the results of marketing campaigns? {#measure}

#### Marketing campaigns

A marketing campaign is the unique combination of a context and a message. Each context you choose to deliver messages can have 1 or several marketing campaigns. For example, posting 3 posts on Facebook and publishing 1 video on TikTok is running 4 different marketing campaign within the same context: using community platforms.

#### Metrics to measure

For each marketing campaign, there are 3 basic metrics you should try to monitor:

1. The number of people reached by the campaign: the reach.
2. The number of people who acted thanks to the campaign: the action rate.
3. The Return On Investment of the campaign: the ROI. How much did it cost and how many users or revenue did it bring?

Each one of this number can be either very precise or largely approximate, either easy or hard to measure. This is true even in digital marketing. But the challenge is to try to have these numbers.

#### Evolution and comparison

The goal is to assess how these numbers:

1. evolve through time: it's the evolution factor.
2. can be compared between different campaigns: it's the comparison factor.

Once you have these metrics, you can:

- rank contexts relatively to one another, and favor the contexts with the best performing campaigns.
- rank campaigns relatively within the same context, and favor the campaigns and the messages with the best results.

## 5. The irrigation framework {#irrigation-framework}

In this chapter, we revisit everything we have explored so far, but in a different way. This results in a practical framework for entrepreneurs: the irrigation framework (IF).

### 5.1. Prerequisites {#prerequisites}

#### Goal

The (ambitious) goal of the Irrigation Framework (IF) is to help you succeed the marketing of your business or your project by listing and order precisely the tasks to perform.

#### Assumptions

The IF is based on the following assumptions:

1. The goal of marketing is to communicate the right information about your offering to the right people at the right time.

2. Choosing how to deliver information is very similar to choosing which part of an irrigation system you want to build and use. The key is to know which pipelines and valves (contexts) to open or close for the water (information or messages) to arrive to the right plants (people), at an optimal quality and quantity. Hence the name of this framework: the Irrigation framework.

#### How to use it?

The IF is a complete list of options and possibilities. It is like a list of useful ingredients to choose from to prepare a meal.

You don't have to use all the ingredients, but, most importantly, you shouldn't. Some of them are almost mandatory (like water or salt), but others are optional. You choose what seems to be relevant to what you're trying to cook. I've indicated what I think is required or recommended between parentheses. Please note that you may disagree with me, and you should feel free to do so.

#### Tasks

There are 3 tasks in the IF:

1. Define your brand and your offering.
2. Capitalize on your users.
3. Craft optimized messages for your pipelines.

I categorize each task as either required, recommended or neutral. Here are the colors I use in the tables below:

- 🟥: required.
- 🟨: recommended.
- ⬛: neutral.
